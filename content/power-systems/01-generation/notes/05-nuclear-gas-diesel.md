## Nuclear power plants

### Where the energy comes from: fission

When a slow neutron hits a nucleus of **uranium-235**, the nucleus splits (**fission**) into two smaller nuclei. A little mass disappears and appears as a huge amount of heat ($E = mc^2$). Each fission also throws out **2 or 3 new neutrons**. These can split more U-235 nuclei, which give more neutrons, and so on: a **chain reaction**.

One kilogram of U-235 gives about as much heat as roughly 2–3 million kilograms of coal. That is why a nuclear station needs very little fuel.

In a reactor the chain reaction must stay **steady**: on average exactly one neutron from each fission should cause the next fission. The parts of the reactor are designed for this.

### Parts of a nuclear reactor

| Part | Job | Materials |
|---|---|---|
| **Fuel** | Fissile material that splits | U-235 (natural or enriched uranium), Pu-239; thorium is *fertile* (becomes U-233) |
| **Moderator** | **Slows down** the fast neutrons so they can cause fission | Heavy water (D₂O), graphite, ordinary water, beryllium |
| **Control rods** | **Absorb** neutrons to control (or stop) the chain reaction | **Boron**, **cadmium**, hafnium |
| **Coolant** | Carries the heat from the core to the heat exchanger | Ordinary water, heavy water, CO₂ gas, liquid sodium |
| **Reflector** | Sends escaping neutrons back into the core | Beryllium, graphite |
| **Shielding** | Protects people from radiation | Thick concrete, lead, steel |

> **Remember:** the moderator **slows** neutrons; the control rods **absorb** them. Pushing the rods deeper absorbs more neutrons and slows the reaction; pulling them out speeds it up.

> **Trap:** heavy water and beryllium are **moderators** (beryllium also a reflector), not control materials. Thorium is a **fuel** (fertile). Boron and cadmium are the control materials.

The heat from the coolant makes steam in a **heat exchanger (steam generator)**, and from there the plant is like a steam station: turbine, alternator, condenser.

### Types of reactors (in short)

- **PWR (pressurised water reactor):** ordinary water as moderator and coolant, kept under high pressure so it does not boil; enriched uranium.
- **BWR (boiling water reactor):** water boils in the core itself and the steam goes straight to the turbine.
- **PHWR (pressurised heavy water reactor):** **natural uranium** with **heavy water** as moderator and coolant. Most Indian reactors are PHWRs (the first stage of India's three-stage nuclear programme).
- **FBR (fast breeder reactor):** **no moderator** (it uses fast neutrons), liquid **sodium** coolant. It "breeds" new fuel (Pu-239 from U-238) while working.

### Advantages and disadvantages

| Advantages | Disadvantages |
|---|---|
| Very little fuel; low fuel cost per unit | **Very high capital cost** |
| No smoke, no CO₂ | Radioactive waste; safety and radiation risk |
| Large output from a small site | Cannot change its output quickly; needs a steady load |
| Can be built far from coal mines | Needs skilled staff; must be away from towns |

A nuclear plant has very high fixed cost and very low running cost, and it should run steadily: it is a **base load** plant.

Note: uranium is a mineral that will run out, so nuclear energy is **not** a renewable source (even though it is low-carbon).

## Gas turbine power plants

A gas turbine works on the **Brayton (Joule) cycle**:

1. A **compressor** compresses air.
2. In the **combustion chamber**, fuel (natural gas or oil) burns in this air.
3. The very hot, high-pressure gases expand through the **turbine**, which drives both the compressor and the alternator.
4. The exhaust leaves at about 500–600 °C.

Features:

- **Starts very quickly** (a few minutes) and takes load fast.
- Small, light, **low capital cost**, little water needed.
- **High fuel cost** (gas/oil) and low efficiency in simple (open) cycle, about 25–35 %.

So the open-cycle gas turbine is the classic **peak load** plant.

**Combined cycle:** the hot exhaust of the gas turbine is used in a waste-heat boiler (HRSG) to make steam for a steam turbine. The overall efficiency rises to about 50–60 %. A combined-cycle plant can be used for base or intermediate load.

## Diesel power plants

A diesel engine drives an alternator.

- **Quick start** (a minute or two), simple, small and easy to install anywhere.
- **Fuel (diesel oil) is costly**, and the capacity is small (a few MW).
- Used as **standby** and **emergency** supply (hospitals, factories, telephone exchanges), for **peak loads** in small systems, and for isolated places where there is no grid.

## Comparison at a glance

| Plant | Capital cost | Running cost | Starting time | Best use |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Steam (coal) | Medium | Medium-high | Hours | Base load |
| Hydro (storage) | Very high | Very low | Minutes | Peak / base (season) |
| Nuclear | Highest | Lowest | Very slow | Base load |
| Gas turbine | Low | High | Minutes | Peak load |
| Diesel | Low | Highest | Minutes | Standby / peak (small) |

## Summary of this topic

- Fission of U-235 by slow neutrons; chain reaction.
- Moderator slows neutrons (heavy water, graphite, water); control rods absorb neutrons (boron, cadmium); coolant carries heat (water, CO₂, sodium).
- India: PHWR (natural uranium + heavy water); FBR has no moderator.
- Nuclear = base load; gas turbine and diesel = peak load / standby.
